# Development Notes

These development notes cover the decisions I made while building this project, why I made them, and what I learned along the way. I wrote them because a lot of these decisions are not obvious from reading the findings one at a time, since each finding only shows the part of the story that belongs to its own technique. The notes are also a way of shedding some light on what the work actually looked like, and the heatmap at the end, built from my Wazuh manager's logs, shows that it took a little over a month.

The reason these decisions cannot be read off the repository is that the repository is the published output rather than the place the work happened. My working notes are a separate document that I am not publishing, and the first two commits both land on the same evening in August, so the commit history records when things were published rather than when they were done. Everything below is written retrospectively, and this version covers the detection engineering half of the project. I will come back and extend it once the AI triage is built, and most likely edit this file a lot more. At this point this file serves as rough notes of my project's decisions rather than shaped thoughts.

**This file is not part of the published output. If you are looking for the rules or the findings themselves, they are not here, and the only things you will find are what went into building them and a receipt that the work took as long as it did.**


## The Original Vision

My original vision was fairly small. I wanted to do a SOC deployment with Wazuh, take it a step further with some AI-assisted triage, and write a few detection rules based on concepts I had picked up from LetsDefend, things like brute force, SQL injection, command injection and XSS. That was about all of it, and the rest of the timeline shows how well that plan held up.

The AI triage was in there for two reasons. The first is that a SOC deployment is the fundamental blue team student project, and I wanted to take mine a step past that by implementing something newer. I had heard quite a bit about AI in the cybersecurity space and the idea of an agentic SOC is not a new one, but I did not know the technical details of how you would actually wire something like that into a SIEM. Rather than research it deeply, I thought that since I was deploying a SOC anyway, I might as well experiment and try to build it myself. The second reason is that the idea came out of the analyst work rather than out of the technology being new, because when I was working through LetsDefend's SOC simulator I could see places where having something like AI in the loop would speed the work up.

There is no AI triage in this repository (yet), and the reason is that the detection engineering took way longer than I expected it to. Writing the rules was only part of that, because most of the time went into documenting them and making sure they were actually polished, which is what the rest of this file is about. The triage is still what I intend to build next, since these six rules produce exactly the alerts it would be working on.


## Rule Week — August 19 to 25

I officially started working on the project on August 17, and the first two days went into configuring everything and getting it working. Configuring everything meant creating the virtual machines, installing the Wazuh manager, getting the agent onto the endpoint and setting up Sysmon there as well. The actual rule writing ran from August 19 to August 25, and I was learning the rule syntax as I went.

The first rule I ever wrote was 100100, and I wrote it to get the gist of how Wazuh rules worked rather than to detect anything in particular. Because I wrote it for the sake of learning, it explains why the first version was really simple, and why it was written against one single command I was testing with rather than against a technique. I considered it my "tutorial rule," and I planned to scrap it later on.

While I was working on 100100 I was also doing some research on the side, and part of that was looking at other home labs I found online. A lot of them had written rules for the same concepts I was planning to cover, brute force, SQL injection and the rest of that LetsDefend list, and on top of that Wazuh already ships rules for most of them. Because I found the same rules everywhere, I started asking myself what good my project would be if it only did what everyone else had already done, so I decided to write rules for things that did not have detections yet. That decision got me thinking about how I could detect actual techniques, ones that might not have an exact rule written for them but were still real enough to count as an attack, and looking for those is how I stumbled into Atomic Red Team.

Because I considered 100100 my tutorial rule, rules 100102 through 100106 are what I consider the actual ruleset, and there were a few decisions that shaped this. The first was that I did not want to keep writing rules in the same exact shape against one Atomic Red Team technique after another. Rules 100100, 100102, and 100103 all read from the command line, so if I kept writing rules with that kind of shape, it would be like farming rules to pad the count rather than learning something new from each one. After those three, I deliberately went for techniques that looked different, so 100104, 100105, and 100106 all involve the Kali Linux machine, either as the attacker or as the other end of a connection. Another important decision I made was that I wanted the rules to be cohesive and follow a narrative, rather than just cherry picking whichever techniques were the easiest to detect. Wanting that narrative is why I picked techniques that sit at different stages of one attack chain, from getting in with a brute-forced login, through running encoded and obfuscated commands and impairing the endpoint's defenses, to calling out over an unusual port and pulling tools down. That choice is also why the findings are numbered by their position in the attack chain, rather than by the order I wrote the rules.

By August 25 all six rules were written and I thought they were good to go, although I was aware of a few weaknesses in them. I expected to make some minor tweaks and fixes while I was writing them up, and the plan at that point was to write the rules up more or less as they stood. That evening I created this repository and committed all six rules.


## The Writeup Phase — August 25 Onward

This was the part of the project I personally found the hardest, because it was where the work turned from writing rules into consolidating them into findings, and most of it became heavy documentation alongside repeated test runs to push each rule further. Those test runs are why a lot of the alert activity shown later in this file was produced long after the rules themselves were written. Finding 01 alone took three days, since it was the one that set the voice and the template every later finding follows, and most of the findings after it took about a day each. It was also where I realized, in hindsight, that the rules were far from polished. Revising the first and second findings showed me that some of them had quite glaring weaknesses, and those weaknesses are why I now think of everything I wrote during rule week as proof of concept rules. The first versions were closer to ideas than finished rules, and the versions that came after them are what I consider the polished ones.

That realization is why most of the rules went through several versions. Each finding explains why I picked the technique, shows the gap the shipped ruleset left, and shows my rule's result against it. The question each finding asks is one I set myself. For 100100 it was whether the rule could catch an encoded PowerShell command, and for the rules written against Atomic Red Team it was whether the rule could catch the technique I was trying to detect. Version 1 of each rule is my first answer to that question, and after that I stress tested it further to get more coverage and showed the new results, so each finding doubled as the writeup and as the place where the final iteration of the rule was actually made.

The same hindsight is what changed my mind about 100100. Instead of scrapping my tutorial rule, I decided to test it against variations of the technique rather than the one command I first wrote it for, and it ended up with three versions. A proper encoded PowerShell rule written against the Atomic Red Team technique would have been 100101, and because I kept 100100 instead, that rule was never written. At the other end of the set, 100106, the last rule I wrote during rule week, came out well enough that it never needed a second version. The final version from each finding is what sits in `local_rules.xml` now, and the README came last, on September 20 and 21.
